One note on how this is written. The mechanisms don't change — how a bracket works, what an apron forbids. The numbers change every single year. So the mechanisms are explained in full below, and every current dollar figure sits in one dated block at the bottom. If the figures look old, they are. The rules above them still hold.

1. THE FOUR LINES

Team payroll gets measured against four thresholds, in ascending order.

The salary cap. A soft cap — teams routinely exceed it using exceptions. Crossing it doesn't cost you money; it costs you the ability to sign free agents with cap space.

The luxury tax line. Above this you pay a penalty on every dollar. This is where money starts.

The first apron. Above this, certain roster tools switch off.

The second apron. Above this, more tools switch off, and the restrictions get severe.

The single most useful thing to understand: the aprons are not taxes. They cost you nothing directly. They take away your ability to do things. A team can be very rich and still trapped, because money isn't the constraint — permission is.

2. THE TAX IS A STAIRCASE, NOT A LINE

This one explains more outcomes than any other rule in the agreement.

The luxury tax is charged in brackets, and each bracket costs more per dollar than the one below it. The first few million over the line are taxed lightly. The next few, more. Keep going and the rate climbs at every step.

Which means the cost of a contract has almost nothing to do with the contract. A six-million-dollar player signed by a team just under the line costs about six million dollars. The identical player signed by a team already deep into the tax can cost thirty million or more, because his salary lands on the expensive steps.

When you see a team refuse to keep a good player on a modest deal, this is almost always why. They aren't cheap. They're standing high on the staircase.

3. THE REPEATER

Pay the tax in three of the previous four seasons and you become a repeater. Every bracket then charges you a steeper rate.

Note what that means: the same roster costs more because of what you did in earlier years. Nothing about the current team changed.

And one season under the line does not clear it. Because the rule looks back four years, a team that paid the tax three years running and then ducks once is still at three of four. It takes two consecutive seasons under the tax line to break repeater status — the first year only stops the bleeding, the second year resets the clock.

That arithmetic explains a lot of behaviour that otherwise looks irrational: a contender making itself slightly worse at a trade deadline, then doing it again the following year.

4. WHAT THE APRONS ACTUALLY FORBID

Above the first apron, a team cannot: - Use the non-taxpayer mid-level exception — it is limited to the smaller taxpayer mid-level - Use the bi-annual exception - Acquire a player via sign-and-trade - Take back more salary than it sends out in a trade - Sign a player who was waived if his pre-waiver salary was above the non-taxpayer mid-level

Note the loop in that first pair: using the non-taxpayer mid-level or the bi-annual exception is itself one of the things that hard-caps a team at the first apron. The tools and the line define each other.

Above the second apron, all of the above, plus: - Cannot use any mid-level exception at all — minimum contracts and re-signing its own players are essentially the only routes left - Cannot combine two or more salaries into one trade - Cannot use a trade exception created by signing and trading away its own player, or certain pre-existing exceptions from earlier seasons - Cannot send cash in trades - Future first-round picks get frozen. Finish a season above the second apron and the team's first-rounder seven years out is immediately locked and cannot be traded. If the team then finishes above the second apron in two of the following four seasons, that pick is permanently moved to the end of the first round — the thirtieth selection — no matter how bad the team is by then. Stay under in three of those four and the pick unfreezes at its natural slot.

That last one is the real deterrent. A team can absorb a tax bill. Losing the ability to trade its own draft picks removes the exit from a bad roster.

5. TRADE EXCEPTIONS

When a team sends out more salary than it takes back, it gets a credit — a trade exception — equal to the difference. It can use that credit later to absorb a salary without sending anything back. They typically expire after a year.

A sign-and-trade creates one too, worth a share of the departing player's new contract.

The catch that traps teams: a second-apron team cannot use most trade exceptions at all. So the asset exists, gets reported, and cannot be spent. It unlocks only by getting under the second apron — and using it then hard-caps the team at that same line for the rest of the season.

6. RESTRICTED FREE AGENCY

A restricted free agent can negotiate with any team, but his current team has the right to match any offer he signs and keep him.

The offer sheet is therefore a weapon. A rival can't usually steal the player — but it can decide what he costs. Send an offer to a player on a team deep in the tax and one of two things happens: the team declines and you get a good player cheap, or the team matches and takes on a tax bill several times the size of the salary. There is no branch where the team sending the offer loses.

The qualifying offer is the alternative: a one-year deal the player can accept instead. It pays far less, but at the end of it he becomes an unrestricted free agent — no matching rights, no return. Players use it as leverage, and occasionally they mean it.

7. WAIVE, STRETCH, AND SET-OFF

Waiving a player releases him. The team still owes the guaranteed money.

Stretching spreads that remaining money across more years, which lowers the annual cap hit and can drop a team under the tax line — while extending the obligation years into the future, often past the point the roster resembles anything current.

Important and frequently confused: waiving and stretching do different things. Removing the salary can clear the aprons. Stretching mainly helps with the tax. They are not interchangeable.

Set-off reduces what the old team owes when the player signs elsewhere — but only by a portion of the new salary, not all of it.

8. THE MINIMUM CONTRACT REIMBURSEMENT

A veteran on a one-year minimum contract counts against the cap at a reduced figure, because the league reimburses the team for the difference.

A two-year minimum gets no reimbursement. The team pays the whole thing.

So an identical player, at an identical salary, costs a team significantly more if the deal runs two years instead of one. Teams operating in tight space have lost real money to this without appearing to spend anything.

9. TRADE BONUSES AND NO-TRADE CLAUSES

A trade bonus (or trade kicker) pays a player a percentage of his remaining salary if he's dealt. That bonus counts as salary in the trade, which can make a player harder to move — sometimes so much harder that the player has to waive it for the deal to work at all.

A no-trade clause requires the player's consent. Very few players qualify to negotiate one.

There is also an implicit no-trade situation: a player who re-signs with his own team under certain conditions gets the right to veto a trade for that season. It isn't negotiated — it's automatic, and it surprises people.

10. OWNERSHIP

Not everything is the CBA. A few structural rules come up repeatedly:

  • Every franchise designates one controlling owner — its governor — who represents it on the Board of Governors, and that person must hold a minimum ownership percentage of the team.
  • Any transfer of ownership requires approval by a supermajority of the thirty governors.
  • Arena leases are municipal contracts, not league documents. They're public records, they run for decades, and they contain the obligations nobody reads.

The tax line is always 121.5% of the salary cap. That's the formula, not a coincidence — $164,961,000 × 1.215 = $200,428,000.

Tax brackets are $6,064,000 wide this season. They started at $5,000,000 in 2023-24 and rise with the cap every year, so anyone quoting a flat $5 million bracket is working from old numbers.

Standard tax rates, per dollar, by bracket above the line: $1.00 · $1.25 · $3.50 · $4.75, then rising $0.50 per bracket ($5.25, $5.75, and so on).

Repeater rates, per dollar, by bracket: $3.00 · $3.25 · $5.50 · $6.75, then rising $0.50 per bracket ($7.25, $7.75, and so on).

Two figures still circulating that are out of date: $1.50 and $1.75 for the first two standard brackets, and $2.50 and $2.75 for the first two repeater brackets. Both were superseded for the 2025-26 season.

The cap is tied to league revenue and cannot move more than 10% in either direction from one season to the next, so every figure above changes each summer.
